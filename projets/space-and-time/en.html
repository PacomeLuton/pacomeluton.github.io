Space and Time

Highlighting relativistic effects around a Schwarzschild black hole

Pacôme LUTON, (LJK, UGA, INRIA)

Shader: https://www.shadertoy.com/view/73tXRM

1Scene and usage

The shader renders in real time a Schwarzschild black hole [1], four planets on circular orbits and a background of stars at infinity, as seen by several observers. Its goal is to show and highlight as many relativistic effects as possible, from both special and general relativity. Each pixel casts a ray backwards through curved spacetime; the ray carries a position and a time. This yields the bending of light, its travel time (including the Shapiro effect [2]), Doppler effects [3], length contraction [4], aberration and the proper time of each object (Lorentz transformation [5]). Each effect has a switch in the Common tab (table 2), which makes it possible to isolate them (section 4); the main constants of the scene can be adjusted (table 3).

Organisation. Common contains the physics (frames, Doppler, orbits, proper time) and the shading; Image contains the ray march; Buffer A stores from one frame to the next the camera orientation (mouse), the chosen observer and the height of the static observer (controls: table 1).

2Three levels of frames

The formulas of special relativity only hold in a frame where the metric is that of Minkowski at the point considered; the shader therefore works on three levels, and each transition involves only one effect.

World \(\leftrightarrow\) static local. The static observer has the same axes as the world; it measures \(d\tau = \sqrt f\,dt\) and radial lengths \(dr/\sqrt f\). With \(\vec k = d\vec x/d\lambda\) the velocity of the ray (ray.dir) [6]:

\[\Delta\vec x_{\text{loc}} = \frac{\Delta x_r}{\sqrt f}\,\vec n + \Delta\vec x_\perp,\]\[\vec\beta = \frac1c\left(\frac{v_r}{f}\,\vec n + \frac{\vec v_\perp}{\sqrt f}\right)\]
(1)

Static local \(\leftrightarrow\) proper. This is special relativity, with the local velocity \(\vec\beta\) of the object and \(\gamma = (1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}\). Lorentz transformations [5]:

\[c\,t' = \gamma\left(c\,t - \vec\beta\cdot\vec x\right),\]\[\vec x\,' = \vec x + (\gamma-1)(\vec x\cdot\hat\beta)\,\hat\beta - \gamma\,\vec\beta\,c\,t.\]
(2)

From these the code derives the contraction of the planets [4], the aberration of directions and the Doppler factor.

3Algorithm

Each box has the colour of the frame in which the computed quantity lives; each annotated arrow is a change of frame.

3.1Clock and camera (steps 1 to 4)

Time flows at the rate of the proper time \(\tau_o\) of the current observer. The factor

\[\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt f}{\gamma}\](gravitational and kinematic dilation [7])
(3)

gives the time \(t_o = \tau_o/(d\tau/dt)_o\) of a fictitious distant observer: this world time is what is used to compute the positions of the objects. The pixel direction is rotated into the world axes (facing the black hole by default), corrected for aberration if the observer is moving, then converted to \(\vec k\) by (1).

3.2Marching (step 5)

Curved rays. As in the Starless raytracer [8], the null geodesic is replaced by Newtonian motion under a fictitious force, with \(\vec L = \vec x\times\vec k\) conserved:

\[\frac{d^2\vec x}{d\lambda^2} = -\frac32\,r_s\,L^2\,\frac{\vec x}{r^5},\]\[\frac{d(ct)}{d\lambda} = -\frac1f.\]
(4)

The trajectory exactly satisfies the Binet equation [9] for photons, \(u'' + u = \tfrac32 r_s u^2\) \((u = 1/r)\). It is integrated with semi-implicit Euler [10] (velocity, then position with the new velocity), and the ray's time goes backwards at each step, which gives the travel time and the Shapiro effect.

Sphere tracing. At each step, each planet is placed at the ray's time and the point is moved into its proper frame, where it is a motionless sphere (branch of step 5); the proper distance \(d'\) is brought back to the world frame by \(d = d'\sqrt f/\gamma\), and the step is \(\min(d/2,\ 0.05\,r)\). The factor \(1/2\) comes from the fact that the planets are moving: while the ray travels \(\ell\), a planet cannot get closer to it by more than \(\ell\), since it moves slower than light. The gap therefore shrinks by at most \(2\ell\), and a step of \(d/2\) never crosses it. The bound \(0.05\,r\) shortens the steps near the black hole, where the curvature (in \(1/r^5\)) varies quickly and where large Euler steps would distort the trajectory.

3.3Planets: orbit

The planets follow circular orbits around the black hole. The shader can speed them up to make the motion visible, but it bounds their local velocity so that it always stays below \(c\).

3.4Doppler (step 6)

The energy of a photon received by an object is computed in the static local frame of its point, with (1) then (2):

\[E = \left(1 + \vec\beta\cdot\hat d\right)\,\gamma\,\frac{1}{\sqrt f},\]\[\mathcal D = \frac{E_o}{E_e},\]
(5)

the three factors (classical, special [3], gravitational [11]) being enabled separately by the three DOPPLER_* macros. \(E_o\) is evaluated at the camera, \(E_e\) at the hit point with the velocity of the planet at the emission time; for the stars, motionless at infinity, \(E_e = 1\).

3.5Proper time and pulsation

The proper time [12] of a planet at the hit point is \(\tau = (d\tau/dt)\,t_e\), with \(t_e\) the emission time carried by the ray. To make the proper time of each object visible, we added a pulsation: \(\tau\) animates the texture and a pulsation \(p(\tau) = e^{-8\,\operatorname{frac}(\tau/T)}\) added to the colour; a disc at the bottom left beats with the same period in the observer's proper time. Since light transmits beats like any other frequency, a planet beats, as seen by the observer, \(\mathcal D\) times faster than this metronome (all effects enabled): blueshifted planets beat faster, redshifted ones more slowly.

3.6Shading (step 7)

Stars. Technique close to [13]. The sky is divided into cells, and one star is placed randomly in each cell. Temperature drawn between 3000 and 10,000 K, colour from Planck's law [14] sampled at (610, 550, 465) nm. The Doppler effect only changes the temperature, because \(\mathcal D^3 L_\nu(\nu/\mathcal D, T) = L_\nu(\nu, \mathcal D T)\). Since the actual luminous gain spans several orders of magnitude, hue is separated from luminance and the gain is strongly compressed (\(\text{gain}^{0.03}\), radius \(\times\ \text{gain}^{0.3}\)). Anti-aliasing: the angular footprint \(e\) of the pixel on the sky is estimated by dFdx/dFdy of the final direction (it includes lensing and aberration); each Gaussian star of radius \(s\) is convolved with the pixel, \(\sigma^2 = s^2 + (e/2)^2\), with amplitude \((s^2/\sigma^2)^{1/2}\) (a visual choice, which does not conserve flux).

Planets. Spherical coordinates of the point in the proper frame, warped by ten alternating area-preserving shears (fluid look, animated by \(\tau\), technique inspired by [15]), then a cosine palette. They are lit by an average star whose hue is that of a black body at \(5500\,\mathcal D\) K: only the hue carries the Doppler effect, the \(\mathcal D^3\) intensity is omitted.

4Some observable effects

The reader is invited to play with the shader: changing the observer, moving the camera, toggling the effects or modifying the constants is the best way to build an intuition for relativity.

5Discussion

Kerr black hole. Moving to a rotating black hole [17] is not straightforward, because the whole method relies on the static observer, which acts as an intermediary between the world and each object. Around a Kerr black hole, the dragging of spacetime by the rotation makes it impossible to stay at rest inside the ergosphere: the static observer no longer exists there. It would have to be replaced by an observer rotating with spacetime, and the conversions between frames would no longer be simple \(\sqrt f\) factors.
